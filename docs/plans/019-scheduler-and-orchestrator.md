## What this plan delivers

"Who does what, on which instrument, when", for one or several confirmed workflows at once:

- **Scheduling:** turning schedule requests (018 W6) into a schedule: every step of every unit on an instrument, a person or both, with start and end times, the moves between them, and the margin left on every timing window and handling rule.
- **Inside a workcell and across the lab:** a workcell (the FlexPod with the Echo, PreciseDrop, Spark, Cytomat and MicroSpin) runs part of a workflow while standalone instruments (the Mantis in tissue culture, the STAR, the BlueWasher) and people at the bench do other parts. The first topic of this plan is how those two levels fit together.
- **Science-aware constraints** (000 idea 1.7): handling rules from what is in each plate (HEK293 cells: 30 min out of the incubator) and SOP timing windows bind the schedule; every rejection and every margin names its source.
- **Simulation and the Gantt:** the same engine that plans also simulates, so the Gantt (by instrument, by person, by plate) is a replay of the plan, not a drawing; digital twins (015) supply durations and 3D playback.
- **Booking:** instruments and people on a calendar, working hours, absences, maintenance.
- **Setup instructions:** what to load where, when, drawn from the transfer plans' deck layouts and shown on the 2D deck and later the 3D twin.
- **Live orchestration:** following a run as it happens and re-planning what hasn't started when something runs late.

## Starting point: echo650-twin

Read at commit 59a7a6a. What exists there, and what doesn't:

- **No constraint solver.** Scheduling is deterministic, event-driven greedy dispatch inside a simulator that is the single source of truth: on every completion, start every step whose predecessors are done and whose resources are free (`src/workcell/engine.js`, `src/workcell-v2/runtime.js`, `server/action-resource-kernel.ts`). The forecast (the Gantt) is the same engine run to the end in a hidden copy, so forecast and execution share one code path.
- **Urgency from the science.** In the deadline-aware kernel, dispatch order is slack first (bound minus exposure so far minus this step's duration), then priority, then ID. The exposure kernel (`server/exposure-kernel.ts`) tracks controlled, exposed and unknown time per plate, continuous, per-excursion or cumulative, and returns exact, incomplete or lower-bound results with slack. It doesn't prune schedules itself: slack drives urgency, and a full replay decides whether a candidate passes.
- **Re-planning the pending part** (`server/pending-scheduler.ts`): completed and running work is frozen; a small, bounded set of allowed edits (release times, priorities, optional staging, routes) is tried by forking the kernel and replaying; the best by minimum slack, then makespan, is committed if it is still fresh.
- **Honest failures:** named outcomes (cycle, deadlock, blocked, "search budget used up is not proof of infeasibility"), never invented start times. Deterministic tie-breaks and a replay journal.
- **Transport inside a workcell** is derived: moves are inserted when the next step's site differs, routed over declared sites and handoff nests, with regrips where needed. Delid and relid are only reported as requirements, not inserted.
- **Not built there:** calendars, working hours, people as resources (one `operator` lock on stack loading), people carrying plates, minimum waits between steps (only maximum exposure is enforced), instruments outside the workcell, Cellario hand-off, setup instructions, several experiments sharing a lab (only a scripted demo). Size ceiling measured at 6 robots, 16 processors, 32 plates.
- **Lessons it records:** one instrument's example (the Echo) shaped the first model and had to be undone; search failure is not proof of infeasibility; a lower bound can reject but never approve; never tune timings to pass a deadline; the optimizer rarely helped (the gain came from running things in parallel).

So 019 ports the kernel, the exposure kernel and the pending re-planner for the workcell level, and adds what the lab level needs that was never built: people, calendars, carrying plates between rooms, standalone instruments, several experiments at once.

| S1 | How do scheduling inside a workcell and orchestrating across the lab fit together? | A) Two levels, one engine. The **lab orchestrator** plans every step across the lab: standalone instruments, manual stations, people, and the workcell. Consecutive steps a unit does inside the workcell become a **workcell segment**, which the **workcell scheduler** (the ported echo650 kernel: sites, robot moves, lids) plans in detail and returns exact entry and exit times; the orchestrator uses those to check windows that cross the boundary (HEK293 plate leaves the Mantis, enters the Cytomat) and asks again if they don't fit · B) One flat scheduler with every robot move, site and person in one problem · C) Orchestrator only; a workcell segment is a block with an estimated duration and no detail | ★ **A.** The two levels work at different grains (robot moves in seconds on shared sites, people and rooms in minutes), and echo650's kernel is already good at the first. B blows past the kernel's measured size ceiling as soon as a week of several experiments is in it. C loses the exact times that exposure limits need. |
| S2 | Who runs the workcell on the day, given Cellario runs the FlexPod today? | A) We plan and simulate each workcell segment; on the day the segment is handed to Cellario (through the device gateway, 022), which executes it. Our plan is the prediction; Cellario's log comes back as actual times and the orchestrator re-plans around them. Until 022, a person starts the segment in Cellario from an exported description · B) We drive the workcell's devices directly through the gateway and stop using Cellario · C) Cellario only: we book the workcell for a block and don't simulate inside it | ★ **A.** Cellario is validated on the real FlexPod and we are not; simulation still gives us the exact times and exposure for planning. B means rebuilding and re-validating a working system (product rule 10). C gives the orchestrator nothing to check windows against. |
| S3 | How do plates get between the workcell, standalone instruments and rooms? | A) The orchestrator derives **carry tasks**: a person takes the plate from one place to the next (workcell load or unload point, instrument, bench, incubator), with a time from a lab travel table (room to room, workcell hotel to bench), defaults marked estimated and replaced by measured times from runs. A carry needs a free person, counts as time out of controlled conditions, and shows on the Gantt and in the run checklist. Inside the workcell, moves stay the robot's (S1) · B) A fixed buffer (say 10 min) between any two steps on different instruments · C) Ignore transport outside the workcell | ★ **A.** Carrying live cells across the corridor is exactly where exposure limits bite, so it has to be a real, timed task. B hides who carries and when; C makes every cross-room schedule optimistic. |
| S4 | How are people scheduled? | A) People are resources: each has working hours, absences and which instruments and stations they are trained on (Mantis, biosafety cabinet, Cellario). Manual steps and carries need one qualified person; the scheduler suggests who and a person can pin it. Nobody is booked outside their hours unless they agree to it on that schedule · B) An anonymous number of operators per shift · C) People are not scheduled; manual steps happen whenever | ★ **A.** In a four-person lab, "who" is the constraint (only the postdoc seeds cells), and the ELISA is mostly manual. B can't tell the postdoc from the automation engineer; C gives schedules nobody can follow. |
| S5 | Is a person needed for the whole of an instrument step? | A) No. Each step's time is split into what needs a person (set up, load, unload, check) and what the instrument does alone. The person is booked only for their part, so one person can start the STAR and go seed plates. The split comes from the instrument kind or method (defaults marked estimated, twins and run records refine it). How long a finished plate may sit on a deck is its handling rule, not a guess · B) Yes, a person is booked for the whole step · C) Only manual steps need people | ★ **A.** Walk-away time is what makes a day with automation fit; B books the postdoc for four hours of Echo runs, C forgets that someone loads the plates. |
| S6 | What engine plans the schedule? | A) Port echo650's deterministic event-driven dispatch: slack-first urgency from the exposure kernel, then priority, then ID; add minimum and maximum waits between steps (echo650 enforced only maximum exposure) and people and calendars; the same engine replays the plan as the simulation for the Gantt; named failures, replay journal. Keep a solver interface so an optimiser (CP-SAT in the science service) can be added later and judged by the same simulator · B) CP-SAT (OR-Tools) in the science service from the start · C) The agent proposes a schedule and code only checks it | ★ **A.** Deterministic and explainable ("the Mantis was busy until 10:40"), already written and tested, and one engine for planning and simulating. A solver may win on multi-day packing later, but echo650 found the gains small next to running things in parallel. C is the agent-guessing we avoid (ADR 0024): the agent asks for options and explains them. |

| S7 | Where do bookings and calendars live? | A) In the app: it is the source of truth for bookings of instruments, transporters and people, maintenance and service windows (008 status and service dates), working hours, lab closures. Each person and instrument gets a read-only calendar feed (iCal) to see their tasks in Outlook or Google; importing personal busy times from those calendars comes later · B) Two-way sync with Outlook or Google from the start · C) An external calendar (Outlook or a booking system) is the source; the app reads it | ★ **A.** The scheduler needs bookings it controls and can re-plan; two-way sync is a large integration with conflict rules, and nothing in the lab depends on it yet. | S8 | Is a schedule a design that a person confirms? | A) Yes. The scheduler drafts a schedule for one or more schedule requests; the page shows it as a Gantt with a readiness panel (every margin on a window or handling rule, what was assumed or estimated, conflicts, who is booked outside their hours). Draft bookings show as tentative to everyone. Confirming books the instruments, transporters and people and puts the runs (013) in "scheduled". A re-plan is a new version with a diff · B) Scheduling books immediately; people undo what they don't like · C) People book by hand; the scheduler only suggests | ★ **A.** Same rule as every design (product rule 3): the agent or engine drafts, a person confirms, and downstream (run checklists, Cellario hand-off) uses confirmed versions. Tentative bookings stop two people planning into the same Cytomat slot. |
| S9 | How do several experiments and routines share the lab? | A) Confirmed bookings stay put; a new request is scheduled into what is free around them. Each request has a priority (urgent, normal, flexible) and an optional deadline. When a new request only fits by moving existing work, the scheduler proposes a re-plan of confirmed but not started bookings, showing whose work moves and by how much, and the owners of the moved work confirm it. Routines (passaging, reagent prep) are recurring requests with a flexibility window ("Monday or Tuesday morning") · B) Every new request re-plans everything not yet started, automatically · C) First come, first served; nothing already booked ever moves | ★ **A.** People plan their week around a confirmed schedule, so it must not shift under them silently (B); but a hard freeze (C) lets a low-priority routine block an urgent screen. Asking the affected owners keeps it fair in a shared lab. |
| S10 | What do agents do in scheduling? | A) They call the scheduler's operations like a person: `schedules.options` (ranked alternatives with numbers: earliest finish, fewest person-hours, most margin, least weekend work), what-ifs ("start Tuesday", "use the Vantage instead of the STAR", "Sam is off Friday"), `schedules.explain` ("the Mantis was busy until 10:40"), drafting a schedule and proposing a re-plan. They set inputs (not before, deadline, pin an instrument or a person), never start times by hand. When a rule is missing or unclear they research it (018 W8) and propose it with citations · B) Agents may edit start times directly and code checks them · C) Agents only explain; people schedule | ★ **A.** Times come from the engine, as numbers come from calculators (ADR 0024); the agent's value is in asking the right what-ifs and explaining the trade-off. B invites plausible but wrong times; C wastes the agent. |
| S11 | Can a constraint be overridden, and by whom? | A) The engine never breaks a hard constraint (a confirmed handling rule, a hard timing window). A person can loosen one for one schedule with a reason ("HEK293 up to 40 min today, cells are robust at this passage"); the loosening is recorded, shown on the Gantt, carried into the run and the notebook, and never changes the rule itself. Agents may propose a loosening, never apply it. Unconfirmed (assumed) rules and soft windows are advice: the schedule shows the margin or the breach as a warning and ranks options by it · B) No overrides; change the rule itself · C) Agents may loosen with a reason too | ★ **A.** Scientists do make informed exceptions, and 000 idea 1.7 promised overrides with a reason; keeping them per schedule stops one bad day from weakening the rule for everyone. |
| S12 | How are transporters modelled (Wali's S3 note)? | A) "Move labware between places" is a capability with providers: a person, an AMR, a track or conveyor, and a workcell's own robot (inside the workcell only). Each transporter is registered like an instrument (008, an AMR is an instrument kind with docking points) and declares where it can go: a map of places (rooms, benches, instrument load points, workcell hotels, AMR docks) with travel times, how many plates it carries, and which labware and conditions it can handle (a person with an ice box, an AMR without temperature control). The orchestrator picks a transporter per carry like it picks an instrument, and multi-leg routes (AMR to the tissue-culture door, person inside) are allowed. People are the only transporter in the seed lab · B) People only now; AMRs added later as a special case · C) One generic "transport" resource with a fixed time | ★ **A.** One model now costs little because people already need the map and travel times (S3), and an AMR then arrives as data, not new code. Conditions on the transporter matter for exposure: cells in a heated carrier aren't "out of the incubator" the same way. |
